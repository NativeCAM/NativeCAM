# Stop machining scrap before chasing rapids

NativeCAM should first remove cutting that achieves nothing. Smarter rapids come later. On the two demo jobs, rapids are only **4–7% of acceleration-modelled run time**, and all 48 in-operation retract links together cost **98 seconds**. By contrast, 3D roughing and finishing over stock that the cut-out throws away takes about **44% of the relief job**, and the V-carve's "corner" spikes leave **55% of its feed length cutting air**. Four small changes were measured, each applied where it is relevant: clip 3D work to the cut-out outline plus a margin, require a 10° turn before a V-carve rises at a convex corner, cap only the Z component of steep descents, and fix the post's start move. Together with short retracts, they cut the relief **37% (23:10 → 14:29)** and the sign **16.5% (11:18 → 9:26)**, with the carved surfaces unchanged to within 0.005 in. The next lever is data, not code. The starter library runs the 1/4 in end mill at **0.0042 in per tooth, below every published plywood floor**, and its 0.5×D stepdown is half the 1×D that router charts assume. Deeper passes alone save **3:19 on the sign (29% of its baseline)**, and a chart-level chip load should also cut cooler. After that, NativeCAM needs two shared models before more algorithms. One is an acceleration-aware cycle-time estimator calibrated on the user's PRO6060; real run time exceeds today's constant-velocity estimate by 4–58%, depending on the real acceleration. The other is a Clipper2 cleared-region stock model, which powers rest-aware depths, stay-down links, engagement-based feeds and eventually adaptive clearing. TSP ordering, adaptive clearing and constant-scallop finishing come last: the evidence shows only seconds of benefit on these jobs, and on a 400 IPM stepper gantry adaptive clearing often runs slower than offset pocketing. All of it can be built clean-room. Clipper2, CavalierContours, OR-Tools and VROOM are safe to ship; FreeCAD, OpenCAMLib, Grbl, Klipper and LinuxCNC are study-only.

## Waste cutting, not rapids, dominates the measured baseline

The baseline audit re-posted both example projects with the current code and replayed every move three ways. The first is a constant-velocity (CV) sum. The second (model B) is a trapezoidal look-ahead planner at 15 in/s² that stops fully at junctions sharper than 30°. The third is a 0.01 in heightmap removal simulator that uses the real flat, ball and V tool shapes to flag feed moves that remove nothing ("air") or that machine stock outside the finished part ("scrap") ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)). **The sign runs 10:11 at constant velocity and 11:18 with acceleration; the relief runs 18:48 and 23:10.** NativeCAM's own CLI estimate (10:03 and 18:45) is a constant-velocity sum, so it understates both ([Toolpath.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/Toolpath.swift)). Rapids cost 45 s of 678 s on the sign and 63 s of 1,390 s on the relief. Cutting the rapid rate to 300 IPM adds only 7–8 s to either job, so faster or shorter rapids cannot matter much ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)).

The time goes into feed moves that do no useful work. On the relief, **1,040 in of feed (46% of all feed length, 617 s) machines scrap**. The cause is that the `.material` boundary is the full stock rectangle, and no option clips 3D work to a chosen vector such as the cut-out ([Model3DGenerator.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/Model3DGenerator.swift)). On the sign, the V-carve's corner rule sends the bit back to Z0 at every vertex whose turn exceeds 1e-9 radians. Outlines are flattened at a 0.01 mm chord tolerance, so every vertex on a convex curve qualifies ([VCarveGenerator.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/VCarveGenerator.swift)). The D's outer loop is 89.85 in long and only 17% of it removes material, with 96 surface apexes across the carve. A second rule compounds this: any descent steeper than 45° is fed entirely at the plunge rate instead of capping its Z component. That leaves **49.8 in of the V-carve at 30 IPM, 56% of its feed time** ([ToolpathBuilder.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/ToolpathBuilder.swift); [baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)). The relief cut-out also spends its first three depth levels (46% of its length) in air, because roughing and finishing have already lowered the surrounding material to about −0.38 in.

The smaller inefficiencies are real but cheap. Generators built with `startXY: nil` make the post rapid to X0 Y0 before the first cut, costing 26 in of extra rapid on the sign and 11 in on the relief ([AvidMach4Post.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Post/AvidMach4Post.swift)). Every XY link climbs to the full 0.5 in clearance, drill pecks rapid back to Z0.1, pockets and 3D roughing return to clearance at every depth level, and ordering is greedy nearest-neighbour with no improvement pass. Operations are emitted in list order with no tool grouping ([CAMEngine.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/CAMEngine.swift)). Two gaps matter later. The post's safety line is `G00 G94 G20 G17 G90 G40 G49 G80` followed by `G91.1`, with no `G64`. The machine profile carries a rapid rate for estimates (400 IPM) and a maximum feed, but no acceleration at all ([AvidMach4Post.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Post/AvidMach4Post.swift); [Machine.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Model/Machine.swift)).

These figures carry one large uncertainty: the acceleration. The 15 in/s² in model B is an assumption. Avid forum users say Avid's shipped Mach4 X/Y acceleration is **50 in/s²**, and many lower it to 15–35 for V-carving and 3D work ([Avid forum: Mach 4 Acceleration/Deceleration](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/mach-4-acceleration-deceleration/1123); [Avid forum: Mach4-Motor-Accel macro](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/mach4-motor-accel-a-macro-and-post-combo/2505)). At 50 in/s², the acceleration model is only 4% (sign) and 8% (relief) slower than constant velocity. Corner-related savings shrink with it. The length-driven ones do not: the clip still saves 6:39–7:53 even at constant velocity. The CV column is therefore the floor for every saving, and the ranking holds at any acceleration.

## Linking and ordering buy seconds; tool changes cost minutes

### Retract only as high as the material demands

Modern CAM decides each link separately. Fusion's "Minimum Retraction" lifts the tool "to the lowest height where the tool clears the workpiece, plus any specified safe distance". A "Maximum Stay-Down Distance" keeps it at depth across short links inside cleared area ([Autodesk forum quoting the retraction-policy popup](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture-forum/3d-adaptive-adjusting-safe-distance-for-minimum-retraction/td-p/7303220); [Fusion help: Stay-Down Distance](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-CAM/files/GUID2E5ABA80-7240-4F1A-989C-2410C4A0CCE5.htm)). Hobby CAM mostly uses a single Safe Z. Its biggest practitioner-reported win is crude: one Carbide Create user says lowering the retract height and raising the plunge rate halved a roughly 6-hour detailed V-carve ([Carbide 3D community](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/how-to-get-better-toolpath-efficiency/82460)). NativeCAM's demos have little of that pattern left. The short-retract prototype recovers only 4 s on the sign and 17 s on the relief, and no remaining in-operation hop is under 0.5 in ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)). The larger cost is re-entry: NativeCAM plunges at plunge feed from 0.1 in above the stock top even when the floor below is already cut. Reaching the fourth level of 0.75 in plywood at 30 IPM takes about 1.3 s of feed. Rapiding to the previous floor plus 0.04 in first cuts that to about 0.45 s per re-entry. That saving scales with V-carve letters, holes and nested parts, not with these two demos ([ordering and linking notes](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/ordering_linking.md)).

The right design is a per-depth cleared-region model: a Clipper2 union of each finished pass's swept tool footprint. Each link then takes one of three forms. It stays down if its tool-swept segment lies inside area cleared to the current depth and is shorter than a stay-down limit. Otherwise it lifts to the highest remaining material under the link plus a safe distance. It goes to full clearance only at program start and end, around tool changes, and across user keep-out polygons for clamps. Safety has to be encoded, not assumed. Never stay down across a tab or uncleared region. Treat a freed part's kerf as unsafe. Make the safe distance a user setting with a conservative sheet-goods default (0.1–0.25 in), because bowed plywood sits above nominal. The same model fixes level-major pockets. With R regions and L levels, level-first needs about L·R inter-region links, while region-first needs about R, plus step-downs that can helix or ramp inside the region's own cleared footprint. Region-first should be the pocket default. Level-first stays the default for final through-passes and tabbed profiles, where cutting every part's last pass at the end keeps a sheet stiff ([ordering and linking notes](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/ordering_linking.md)). This is harder than it looks. Fusion's "Order by islands" failure was logged as bug CAM-9911 in 2018, and an Autodesk community manager later said fixing it "was going to be a large project. It's been parked" ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-support-forum/order-by-island-not-working/td-p/7943265)). NativeCAM should build region ordering into its generators from the start rather than retrofit it.

### A better tour is worth 5–15% of air travel, not of job time

Every CAM system examined that documents its ordering starts from greedy nearest-neighbour (NN). The better open-source ones add cheap local search. FreeCAD's 2025 `tsp_solver` builds an NN route and then improves it "with 2-opt and relocation moves", with optional fixed start and end points ([FreeCAD tsp_solver.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/main/src/Mod/CAM/App/tsp_solver.cpp)). Kiri:Moto tests every vertex of every remaining polygon as a candidate entry ([Kiri:Moto paths.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GridSpace/grid-apps/master/src/geo/paths.js)). The DIMACS challenge places NN tours more than 15% above the Held–Karp bound ([DIMACS TSP Challenge](https://archive.dimacs.rutgers.edu/Challenges/TSP/results.html)). Measured gains over a *good* construction heuristic are modest: 4.2% in laser cutting ([Dewil et al. 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/tprsxx/v53y2015i6p1761-1776.html)) and 10–15% of machine time from one practitioner's G-code reorder ([Carbide 3D community](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/how-to-get-better-toolpath-efficiency/82460)). The 26–40% figures in drilling and PSO papers are against naive or zig-zag baselines, so NativeCAM should never promise them relative to NN ([Advances in Mechanical Engineering 2017](https://doaj.org/article/7aedb9c8ce664a10942bc2a244e2889d); [JMST 2019](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12206-019-0134-3.pdf)). On the demos, NN is already optimal for the four corner holes, and the V-carve has only five links.

The case for ordering work rests on jobs unlike the demos, plus perceived quality. Carbide Create users call V-carve ordering "entirely too random", and Estlcam users describe carves that cross the whole sign for one detail and come back ([Carbide 3D community](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/how-to-get-better-toolpath-efficiency/82460); [V1E forum](https://forum.v1e.com/t/estlcam-carve-efficiency/8662)). The implementation is small, a few hundred lines of Swift:

- NN built over a grid index;
- 2-opt and Or-opt restricted to 8–10 nearest neighbours;
- an entry-point pass that moves each closed loop's start to the point minimizing distance to its neighbours, which usually falls mid-edge, beating Kiri's vertex-only choice;
- link costs measured in *time*, not distance, because retract and plunge time dominate short links.

Precedence belongs in a hierarchy, not inside the TSP. A Clipper2 PolyTree gives the nesting: holes, pockets and V-carves inside a part are ordered as an open path that ends near the part's own entry, and then the outer profile is cut. A cheap portfolio, borrowed from Vectric's merged toolpaths, is to compute several orderings and keep the fastest under the estimator ([Vectric Merged Toolpath](https://docs.vectric.com/docs/V12.5/Aspire/ENU/Help/form/Create%20Merged%20Toolpath/index.html)).

### Schedule tools first and air second

On the user's machine a tool change is a human stop. The sequence is M6, travel to the manual tool-change position, a collet swap, a touch-plate re-zero and spindle spin-up. Avid's Mach4 profile provides the M6 macro for exactly this, and Avid users place the touch plate under the tool-change position ([Avid Mach4 Users Guide](https://www.avidcnc.com/cad/2020Q3/Mach4_Users_Guide_v2020Q3_1.pdf); [Avid forum: Auto Z touch plate](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/g-codes-for-auto-z-touch-plate-commands/3525)). Each re-zero also risks a visible Z step between surfaces cut by different tools, and Avid's touch-plate script has had a G43 H1 bug when a tool height offset is active ([Avid forum: touch plate bug](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/avid-touch-plate-bug-when-a-tool-height-is-set-and-active/1789?page=2)). A change is measured in minutes, more than every link in both demos combined. The scheduler should therefore minimize tool changes first, estimated time second, and use the user's order as the tie-break.

Build a precedence graph: inside features before their part's cut-out, roughing before finishing, clearance tool before V-bit, large tool before rest tool. Then run a ready-list or exact search; jobs rarely exceed 20 operations. Merge adjacent same-tool operations so M6 appears only when the tool number changes. Never insert a change to save air time. D'Souza's warning applies: linking all same-tool work into one path "can lead to tool crashes in certain situations", so merged links must pass the cleared-region check ([D'Souza 2006](https://research.me.mtu.edu/pubs/D'Souza-%20On%20Setup%20Level%20Tool%20Sequence%20Selection%20for%202.5-D%20Pocket%20Machining.pdf)). The relief shows the hard trade-off. It runs rough (T1), finish (T7), cut-out (T1), which is two changes. Rough, cut-out, finish needs one, but it finishes a part held only by tabs. That should be an explicit, labelled option, not a silent default ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)).

## Mach4 rewards arcs and punishes stops it never documents

### Constant velocity on Mach4 breaks on more than corners

Mach4 does all the planning and corner blending. The Ethernet SmoothStepper only buffers a time-sliced velocity stream at **1,024 slices per second with a 0.18 s FIFO** and turns it into step pulses, so the estimator must model Mach4, not the ESS ([Warp9: Setting up the SmoothStepper and Mach4](https://warp9td.com/index.php/gettingstarted/setting-up-the-smoothstepper-and-mach4); [Warp9 FAQ](https://warp9td.com/index.php/faq/faq-mach4)). Mach4's G64 takes **no P tolerance**, unlike LinuxCNC's ([Machsupport: Mach4 Constant Velocity & Exact Stop](https://www.machsupport.com/forum/index.php?topic=35121.0)). Cornering comes instead from a 180-entry "CV feedrate per angle" table, which the mcCvTuning wizard fills from a tolerance and the motor acceleration ([Machsupport: Stop CV on Angles?](https://www.machsupport.com/forum/index.php?topic=35872.0)). One Avid forum user says "Avid never asked users to run the CV wizards". Another found that setting Exact Stop in Mach4's Defaults tab had no effect, while a G61 in the file did ([Avid forum: Mach4 CV Tuning Wizard](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/mach4-cv-tuning-wizard/3205)). The practical conclusion is that the motion mode lives in the G-code, so NativeCAM's header, which omits G64 today, should state `G64` explicitly and repeat it after every tool change.

Warp9 lists what breaks the CV chain: every G0 ("always in exact stop mode"), G31, G4, S words, T words and "all other macros" except M62–M65 and M67. Repeated positions break it too, and so do moves shorter than one ESS time slice ([Warp9](https://warp9td.com/index.php/gettingstarted/setting-up-the-smoothstepper-and-mach4)). One slice covers 0.041 mm at 100 IPM and 0.165 mm at 400 IPM. A minimum emitted segment of about 2.5 slices, roughly 0.2–0.4 mm at router feeds, is a sensible floor, though the 2.5 margin is judgement rather than a published rule. The poster should also compare *formatted* coordinates and drop blocks that print identically. Warp9 says leaving Mach4's look-ahead at 20 lines "will not interfere with CV". A finite look-ahead must still be able to stop within the distance it can see, so v ≤ √(2·a·N·L). At 50 in/s² and N = 20, that caps 0.1 mm segments near 168 IPM and 0.5 mm segments near 376 IPM. This is general planner behaviour, and Mach4's adherence to it is unverified until timed ([Mach4 motion notes](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/mach4_motion_cycle_time.md)). NativeCAM's current files do not flood the controller: only 1% of feed moves are under 0.01 in. The cost sits in corners instead. 3D roughing has **724 junctions sharper than 30°** on a 0.079 in median segment, which is why its acceleration-modelled time is **1.46× its CV time**, the worst of any toolpath type ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)).

### Blend corners in CAM with an explicit error budget

Mach4's G64 rounding is governed by an untuned, undocumented table, so the deterministic choice is to blend in NativeCAM. At a vertex turning by α, a tangent fillet with allowed deviation e has radius r = e·cos(α/2)/(1 − cos(α/2)). That is the same circle as Grbl's junction-deviation model, so a CAM fillet and a controller junction tolerance spend the same accuracy budget, but the fillet's error is explicit and inspectable ([Grbl planner.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gnea/grbl/master/grbl/planner.c)). On offset-generated paths such as Clipper2 pocket rings, a fillet inside the turn stays within the convex feasible region, so it cannot gouge and leaves at most e of extra material. Linking moves and non-offset paths still need a collision check.

Fillets do not keep the feed, though. Speed through a fillet is capped at √(a·r). At 50 in/s², a 90° corner with e = 0.05 mm allows only about **29 IPM**, and e = 0.127 mm allows about 47 IPM ([Mach4 motion notes](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/mach4_motion_cycle_time.md)). Small fillets remove full stops and their witness marks. Real speed needs larger budgets on roughing (0.1–0.25 mm) or deliberate racetrack corners, while finishing keeps e small, mirroring Fusion's use of smoothing on roughing only ([Fusion smoothing reference](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-CAM/files/GUID5A951723-4797-4F5F-A7A3-45DCBCA2D26F.htm)).

The output pipeline should run in this order:

1. Remove duplicate points after quantization.
2. Merge near-collinear points with Ramer–Douglas–Peucker at no more than half the tolerance.
3. Fit arcs or biarcs on the remaining budget.
4. Enforce the minimum segment length.

Biarcs give tangent-continuous joins, which is ideal for a per-angle CV table. NativeCAM's 0.01 mm arc tolerance is tight for wood and should split into a separate roughing smoothing tolerance (0.025–0.05 mm). The existing conventions are right and should stay: I/J incremental arcs under G91.1, sweeps under 180°, and helices as G2/G3 with Z. Mach4's manual calls centre format "more accurate and reliable" than R ([Mach4 Mill G-code Manual](https://www.machsupport.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Mach4%20Mill%20GCode%20Manual.pdf)). After rounding, the post should verify that start and end radii agree within 0.0005 in, falling back to G1 otherwise. Mach4 documents no spline output, so splines can only ever be internal. One interaction needs care. ArcFitter only fits runs at a constant F, so any future per-segment feed scheduling will fragment arcs unless feeds are quantized and held over minimum run lengths ([ArcFitter.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Toolpath/ArcFitter.swift)).

### Estimate time the way Mach4 will run it

Distance divided by feed underestimates. In the baseline, modelled run time exceeds NativeCAM's figures by about 12% (sign) and 24% (relief) at 15 in/s², and by anywhere from 4% to 58% across the 5–50 in/s² range Avid users run ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)). The literature agrees on the size of the gap and on the cure. Saito et al. measured a path-÷-feed estimate off by **51%** against **7%** for a control-principle model ([Saito, Aoyama, Sano, IJAT 2016](https://www.fujipress.jp/ijat/au/ijate001000030429/)). Ward et al. predict cycle time with better than 90% accuracy by modelling interpolator corner blending ([arXiv 2102.02062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.02062)). A Fusion user found actual time a little over 27% above the estimate ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture-forum/machining-time-estimates-are-junk/td-p/7035875)).

NativeCAM should run a block-level planner over the *posted* blocks, after quantization:

- **Speed caps per block:** the programmed feed, each axis's velocity limit projected onto the move direction, √(a·R) on arcs, and G0 at the motor-tuning maximum velocity.
- **Junction caps:** taken from an imported copy of the Mach4 CV table, or from a Grbl-style junction deviation δ fitted by calibration. They are forced to zero at every G0, S, T, macro, G4 and zero-length block.
- **Look-ahead:** a Grbl-style reverse and forward pass over a sliding 20-block window.
- **Block times:** Klipper's three-phase trapezoid formulas.
- **Fixed overheads:** tool change, touch-off and spin-up.

Sources: [Klipper toolhead.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Klipper3d/klipper/master/klippy/toolhead.py); [Warp9](https://warp9td.com/index.php/gettingstarted/setting-up-the-smoothstepper-and-mach4).

Mach4 motor tuning exposes only velocity and acceleration, with no jerk, so a jerk-limited model such as Ruckig adds nothing for version 1 ([Avid Mach4 Configuration](https://www.avidcnc.com/support/instructions/software/mach4Configuration/); [Ruckig](https://github.com/pantor/ruckig)). The machine profile needs per-axis velocity and acceleration for X, Y and Z, plus the drive tier. The current PRO6060 page lists 250/400/600 IPM cut and 500/800/1,200 IPM rapids for NEMA 23, NEMA 34 and ClearPath servos ([Avid PRO6060](https://www.avidcnc.com/pro6060-5-x-5-cnc-router-kit-p-634.html)). This estimator is more than a display number. It becomes the objective function for link costs, the ordering portfolio, adaptive-versus-offset choices and acceleration recommendations, which is why it comes before any of them.

## Wood wants thick chips, so spindle speed is the real feed knob

Router chip load is feed ÷ (RPM × flutes). Freud's reasoning for wood is thermal: a chip that is too small "does not carry enough heat away" and dulls the carbide early, while one that is too large leaves a rough edge ([Freud CNC feed & speed guide](https://www.freudtools.com/public/assets/freud/downloadables/freudtools-router-bit-feed-and-speed-for-cnc-20170822.pdf)). Against that standard, NativeCAM's starter library is under-fed. The 1/4 in two-flute upcut at 150 IPM and 18,000 RPM runs **0.0042 in per tooth**. Freud's solid-carbide range for 1/4 in is **0.006–0.009 in in plywood**, 0.008–0.011 in in hardwood and 0.013–0.017 in in MDF. An Onsrud-derived chart puts the floor for plywood, MDF and hardwood at 0.005 in ([Freud guide](https://www.freudtools.com/public/assets/freud/downloadables/freudtools-router-bit-feed-and-speed-for-cnc-20170822.pdf); [Artisan's Asylum, citing Onsrud](https://wiki.artisansasylum.com/wiki/ShopBot_Feeds_and_Speeds_and_Chip_Load); [ToolLibrary.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Model/ToolLibrary.swift)). The 1/4 in ball nose at 120 IPM runs 0.0033 in, while Amana charts its 1/4 in ball for wood at 0.007–0.009 in and 250–320 IPM at 18,000 RPM ([Amana Spektra 3D chart](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1558/5651/files/A_Spektra-Coated-3D-Profiling-Feed-Chip-Load-Chart-v7.pdf)). Reaching Freud's plywood minimum of 0.006 in needs either about 216 IPM at 18,000 RPM or about 12,500 RPM at 150 IPM. That is arithmetic on chart values, and the user's material and spindle still need a cut test to confirm it.

Depth tells the same story. The charts assume a depth of cut up to 1×D, derated by 25% at 2×D and 50% at 3×D ([Freud guide](https://www.freudtools.com/public/assets/freud/downloadables/freudtools-router-bit-feed-and-speed-for-cnc-20170822.pdf)). NativeCAM's 0.125 in stepdown on a 1/4 in bit is 0.5×D. The audit's 0.25 in setting saved 3:19 on the sign. Because NativeCAM spreads levels evenly (n = ceil(depth/step)), the 0.77 in cut-out actually takes **four passes of 0.1925 in**. That is close to an Avid PRO4896 owner's practice of passes under 0.20 in in Baltic birch with a 3 HP spindle ([Operation.swift](/Users/dsmith/natcam/Sources/CAMCore/Model/Operation.swift); [Autodesk forum: plywood edge quality](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture/how-to-maximize-plywood-edge-cut-quality-in-fusion-360-cam/td-p/11449015)).

The same even spreading creates a latent defect for compression bits. A compression bit's first pass must reach below its upcut section, typically about 1×D, or it cuts like an upcut and frays the top face ([ToolsToday/Amana](https://toolstoday.com/learn/downcut-upcut-and-compression-bits)). The starter compression bit's 0.25 in stepdown becomes a 0.1925 in first pass on that cut-out. For compression tools the first level should be at least the upcut length plus a margin, with the remaining depth spread across the rest of the levels.

On an Avid PRO the binding limit is usually the drive's cut-speed cap, not the chart. A 1/2 in two-flute bit in MDF wants 828–972 IPM at 18,000 RPM, well above even the ClearPath tier's 600 IPM ([PRO4848 page](https://www.avidcnc.com/pro4848-4-x-4-cnc-router-machine-p-1335.html); [Freud guide](https://www.freudtools.com/public/assets/freud/downloadables/freudtools-router-bit-feed-and-speed-for-cnc-20170822.pdf)). NativeCAM should therefore derive RPM per operation, as RPM = clamp(F_cap/(z·h_target), RPM_min, RPM_max). For example, 400 IPM / (2 × 0.020 in) = 10,000 RPM. Feed then varies only within [burn floor, F_cap]. Many VFD spindles lose power below rated speed, which this research did not confirm for the user's spindle, so the power curve should be a profile input. The consequence is important: chip-thinning feed boosts mostly get clipped at F_cap on a stepper machine. On this machine, engagement-aware feeds are worth more for protecting bits and choosing RPM than for raw speed.

Radial chip thinning and corners are the two engagement effects that matter. Below 50% stepover the real chip is thinner than the programmed feed per tooth. The exact restoring multiplier is 1/sin φ, where φ = arccos(1 − 2·ae/D) is the engagement angle. That is 1.67× at 10% stepover and 1.25× at 20%, and it agrees with Dapra's 1.70× and Helical's formula ([Helical Solutions](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0961/5830/files/Milling-_Angle_of_Engagement_and_Chip_Thinning.pdf); [Dapra](https://www.dapra.com/articles/radial-chip-thinning)). Corners undo it. At a sharp 90° corner of an offset pocket, peak engagement is roughly φ_s + 90°: **about 127° at 10% stepover and 168° at 40%**, nearly a slot. A chip-thinned feed arriving there overloads the tool, which matches Helical's advice to cut feed 30–50% in inside corners ([Helical: Corner Engagement](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0612/0733/files/Milling-_Corner_Engagement.pdf)). The corner formula is my own geometry, not a published result.

The workable feed law caps both peak chip thickness (via 1/sin φ) and removal rate (via a slot calibrated on the user's machine), with a burn floor that triggers a warning rather than a silent slowdown. Engagement can come from the same Clipper2 cleared-region model as stay-down linking. Version 1 can be Fusion-style geometric corner slowdown, with a maximum direction change, a reduced-feed radius, distance and rate ([Fusion Feed Optimization](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-CAM/files/GUIDCFB956EA-793B-4F8A-B487-028219913D96.htm)). Fusion users report that corners built from many short segments evade detection, so NativeCAM should detect curvature across segments ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture/feed-optimization-slows-all-corners-when-quot-only-inner-quot-is/td-p/9238577)). Feed changes should be quantized to about 5% steps with hysteresis and minimum run lengths, which also keeps ArcFitter's runs intact.

Entry strategy is where NativeCAM's 45° plunge-rate rule does its quality damage. The preference order is helix, then ramp, then plunge ([CNCCookbook](https://www.cnccookbook.com/helical-interpolation-ramp-angle-calculator-best-ways-to-enter-a-cut)). Vectric runs "all ramp moves… at the plunge rate", and NativeCAM's rule behaves the same way ([Vectric 2D Profile](https://docs.vectric.com/docs/V12.5/Aspire/ENU/Help/form/uiProfileMachineForm/index.html)). At a 3° ramp, Z then moves at about 5% of the plunge rate while XY crawls, which is a dwell-and-burn condition in wood. An Avid user also blames jerky gantry motion on lead-in and ramp feeds far below cut feed, recommending they stay within about 15% of it ([Avid forum](https://forum.avidcnc.com/t/mach-4-acceleration-deceleration/1123)). The fix is F_ramp = min(k·F_cut, F_plungeZ/sin α), which the Z-cap prototype already validates for time.

Helices should run at 0.5–0.95×D centre diameter with a pitch of π·d·tan α. Below about 0.25×D they should fall back to a zig-zag ramp, and only then to a plunge for centre-cutting tools ([Fusion helix default, Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture-forum/minimum-helix-diameter/td-p/8516994); [FeatureCAM fallback](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/FCAM/files/GUID-2F2383B5-EE1B-41A8-A638-93F291BD3D2C.htm)). No wood-specific manufacturer ramp angle was found. A 2–3° default with a per-tool override is reasonable, and Freud forbids plunge-drilling with carbide-tipped bits. Sheet cut-outs benefit from several more options:

- ramped "3D" tabs, which Vectric says run "faster and smoother" than lift-and-plunge tabs ([Vectric](https://docs.vectric.com/docs/V12.5/Aspire/ENU/Help/form/uiProfileMachineForm/index.html));
- an optional onion skin of about 0.03 in, which WoodWeb shops use ([WoodWeb](https://woodweb.com/knowledge_base/Tabbing_Versus_OnionSkinning_for_Small_Parts.html));
- a finish allowance well under D/3;
- removing the final-level traverse over tabs, which NativeCAM currently cuts in air.

### Adaptive clearing pays only in deep pockets on this gantry

FreeCAD's Adaptive is the only mature open-source engine, and it is a good model of the walk-style algorithm. It keeps the cleared region as integer polygons. It steps the tool forward, searching headings within ±45° until the area cut per unit distance is within 5% of a target equal to the stepover, using at most 30 trials. It computes cut area analytically with a sweep line, and it stays down on links no longer than three times the direct distance ([FreeCAD Adaptive.cpp](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/blob/main/src/Mod/CAM/libarea/Adaptive.cpp); [Adaptive.hpp](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/blob/main/src/Mod/CAM/libarea/Adaptive.hpp)). It was migrated to **Clipper2** in 2026, so its data structures map one-to-one onto NativeCAM's ([FreeCAD commit history](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/commits/main/src/Mod/CAM/libarea/Adaptive.cpp)). It is LGPL-2.1-or-later, so it can be studied and reimplemented but not ported.

Several "engagement milling" patent filings date from 2004–2005, and expiry and claims need counsel ([Justia US 7,451,013](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7451013)). Effort is substantial: 2–4k lines, with robustness work dominating.

The router evidence argues against making it a default. One Onefinity user notes that at the same feeds adaptive "will usually take longer than a normal pocket" because of its non-cutting moves ([Onefinity forum](https://forum.onefinitycnc.com/t/general-questions-on-machining-adaptive-clearing/11409)). One Fusion user's 3D adaptive estimate of 75 minutes ran about 180 ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-360-manufacture/3d-adaptive-clearing-woes/td-p/10142655)). The physics is the √(a·r) cap again. Re-engagement loops of 0.125 in radius max out at **82 IPM at 15 in/s²**, and even at Avid's 50 in/s² a 0.25 in loop reaches only 212 IPM ([adaptive clearing notes](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/adaptive_clearing.md)). The win is real where full-width slotting forces low feeds. One Shapeoko owner ran adaptive at 60 IPM in hardwood where pocketing had to drop to 30–35 IPM ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-manufacture/inefficient-2d-adaptive-clearing-toolpath/m-p/5608904)).

That points to a rule of thumb: offer adaptive for pockets at least 1.5–2×D deep, in hardwood or dense MDF, or with 1/8 in tools. Neither demo qualifies. The sign pocket is shallow, and the relief roughing is four levels to about 0.38 in. Staging should go from corner-limited offset pocketing, to trochoidal slots, to the full walk algorithm, reusing its cleared-region input for layered 3D roughing.

## Relief finishing gains come from boundaries and slope-aware passes

The relief's biggest finishing win is already measured: the finish raster alone spent 563 in (6:06 under the acceleration model) over scrap, and clipping to the cut-out boundary removes nearly all of it. A real implementation needs a "selected vector plus margin" boundary with separate margins for rougher and ball, defaulting on when a cut-out exists. Vectric exposes the same concept as a machining limit of "Selected vectors" with a boundary offset ([Vectric Aspire 3D Finish](https://docs.vectric.com/docs/V12.0/Aspire/ENU/Help/form/Finish%20Machining%20Toolpath/index.html)).

After that, finishing becomes a quality problem with a known geometry. A raster's XY stepover stretches on slopes that rise across the rows, to s/√(1 − sin²θ·sin²φ). Scallop height grows with the square of that spacing. A 1/8 in ball at 10% stepover leaves 0.008 mm on flats, **4× that on 60° slopes across the rows and about 15× at 75°** (my derivation; no published source states the inclined formula). The commercial answers line up with that geometry. Fusion's Steep and Shallow uses a threshold "usually" of 30–45° with an overlap band, Creo defaults to 45°, and Carveco offers Raster X & Y as a "better surface finish, but time-consuming" option ([Autodesk blog: Why Steep and Shallow](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/why-steep-and-shallow/); [PTC Creo](https://support.ptc.com/help/creo/creo_pma/r12/usascii/manufacturing/nc/milling_parameters_specific_to_finishing.html); [Carveco](https://knowledge.carveco.com/portal/en/kb/articles/machine-relief-panel-finishing-options)). A cross-hatch second pass limits the 60° growth to 1.26× but doubles run time. Steep-and-shallow instead spends extra passes only on the steep fraction, with a waterline Z-step of Δz = s·tan θ_t so the scallop stays continuous across the seam. At a 40° default that gives Δz ≈ 0.84·s. Vectric's typical stepover is 8–12% of tip diameter ([Vectric](https://docs.vectric.com/docs/V12.0/Aspire/ENU/Help/form/Finish%20Machining%20Toolpath/index.html)). The largest ball that fits the smallest concave radius, followed by tapered-ball rest machining only where residual stock remains, beats a small tool everywhere ([Loughborough 3D machining notes](https://learn.lboro.ac.uk/ludata/cd/cad/cnc/3dmachining.htm)).

The clean architecture is one cutter-location (CL) height map per tool, from which every strategy derives:

- **Waterline:** marching-squares iso-contours at each Z, refined by bisection with the exact drop-cutter.
- **Steep/shallow mask:** for a ball, the CL map's slope equals the contact-normal slope, so it is thresholded directly.
- **Constant-scallop:** iso-lines of a geodesic distance field. Zou's heat method needs only two sparse linear solves, which Accelerate can do ([Zou 2021, arXiv 2004.09136](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09136)).
- **Pencil:** cells where the winning contact point jumps between faces.
- **Rest:** NativeCAM's own `MaterialSimulator` residual.

OpenCAMLib's fiber-and-weave waterline is exact but complex. FreeCAD's simpler "OCL Dropcutter" waterline builds a topo map from scan lines, which shows a CL-map approach works in practice ([OCL waterline.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aewallin/opencamlib/master/src/algo/waterline.cpp); [FreeCAD Waterline.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/main/src/Mod/CAM/Path/Op/Waterline.py)).

`TriangleDropCutter` has cheap speedups available:

- sort each bin's triangles by maximum Z and stop once no remaining triangle can lift the tool;
- test the previous sample's winning triangle first;
- precompute normals in a structure-of-arrays layout, since `ballDrop` recomputes them per query and loops over array literals in the hot path;
- sample adaptively along rows, using OpenCAMLib's three-point `flat()` test with cosLimit 0.999 ([OCL adaptivepathdropcutter.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aewallin/opencamlib/master/src/dropcutter/adaptivepathdropcutter.cpp)).

It also needs bull-nose and tapered-ball cutters, since tapered balls fall back to the height grid today.

Linking and output decide how a finish cuts on Mach4. The current finish is 17,731 G1 moves on a 0.038 in median, about 50 blocks per second at 120 IPM, with **zero arcs**. The cause is that its 45° rows lie in a vertical plane that is not a principal plane, so no G17, G18 or G19 arc can represent them ([baseline audit](/Users/dsmith/natcam/research_notes/Toolpath%20optimization%20for%20Avid%20CNC/nativecam_baseline.md)). Rows at 0° or 90° could use G18/G19 arcs; a UCCNC user replaced thousands of short moves with "a handful of G2/G3" that way ([CNCDrive forum](https://forum.cncdrive.com/viewtopic.php?p=32866)). Mach4 and ESS handling of G18/G19 is unverified, and grain direction usually dictates the angle, so this stays low priority. Waterline loops lie in XY and are ideal G17 arc candidates.

Higher-value changes are:

- link limits in millimetres plus a maximum Z climb;
- arc turnarounds instead of 180° reversals, each of which forces a full stop;
- rows that start off the model rather than plunging onto it;
- a point-filter tolerance tied to ¼–½ of the target scallop, with a reported lines-per-second figure, because Fusion warns that very tight tolerances cause "data starving" ([Fusion Parallel Finishing](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-CAM/files/GUID17CC1F90-A2A5-4CC3-80EB-B7972E327E0F.htm));
- a full 0–180° raster angle with a stock grain field and one-way or uphill-only options. Practitioners report ±35° crossed passes and along-grain finishing passes reducing torn grain, though the evidence is anecdotal ([Sawmill Creek](https://sawmillcreek.org/threads/roughing-pass-or-no-roughing-pass-for-3d-project.287197/); [Carbide 3D community](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/3d-finish-strange-results/77548)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "toolpath optimization" means for NativeCAM. On a manual-tool-change gantry running Mach4, the expensive mistakes are not elegant-algorithm problems. They are cutting that removes nothing, cutting that removes waste, plunge-rate crawls, and chip loads that burn. The first 37% on the relief needs no new algorithm at all. The real leverage is two pieces of shared infrastructure. A cleared-region stock model on Clipper2 underlies rest-aware depths, stay-down linking, engagement feeds, adaptive clearing and rest machining. A calibrated Mach4 motion model underlies honest estimates, link costs, ordering portfolios and every "is adaptive worth it here" decision. Built once, each turns a family of features from guesswork into a measurable trade.

One machine-specific insight should shape the feeds work. Avid's 250–600 IPM drive caps sit below what chart chip loads demand at 18,000 RPM. On this machine, spindle speed is therefore the real variable for chip load, and engagement-aware feeds pay off mainly by protecting bits and preventing burns, not by going faster. The open question that most changes the numbers is the user's actual acceleration setting. At 50 in/s² the acceleration-sensitive savings roughly halve, while the length-driven ones (clipping, V-carve spikes, stepdown) stand. Reading Mach4's motor tuning and running the timing suite are the cheapest high-value steps on the list.
